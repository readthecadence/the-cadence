## XX. THE MARKET — The Fickle God

Against the true Objective — which is unmeasurable and worthy and patient — there stands its great counterfeit: a god who is himself a number, who prices the worth of a House continuously and in public, and who demands to be appeased not once a year but every ninety days without fail. His name is the Market, and the public Houses live and die by his favor.

**The Crowd with a Ticker.** The Aligned who have read of Vainglory already know this god, for he is only the crowd of the square wearing a second face. Where the crowd in the square votes with its cheers, the Market votes with its coin; but it is the same fickle multitude — as easily swayed, as quick to adore and abandon, as certain to cheer a House gladly toward its ruin and forget it by the morning. The value-seekers of old, wiser than most, drew him as a manic partner who appears each dawn in a wholly new humor and shouts a fresh price upon your worth; and they counseled that he was to be used and never heeded — served at your table, but never seated at its head.

**The Rite of Appeasement.** Four times a year the public House performs the great propitiation, which the plain tongue calls the *earnings call*. The lords of the House stand before the god's interpreters and lay their numbers upon the altar; they read the results and offer their guidance for the season to come; the interpreters press them with sharp questions; and in the hours after, the god renders his verdict — so that a House which beat the whispered expectation by a hair may be lifted up, while a House that grew mightily but by a hair less than was hoped may be cast down and lose a fortune between one breath and the next. For the god judges not by what was built, but by the gap between what was built and what was expected — a stranger and crueler measure than any the faith itself keeps.

**The Sins It Tempts.** The Market does not merely watch; it tempts, and the sins it rewards are precisely those the faith forbids. It tempts the House to short-termism — to sacrifice the decade for the quarter, to burn the seed corn to swell this one season's harvest. It blesses the buyback that flatters the number while the morrow goes hungry. It rewards the watermelon raised to the scale of a whole enterprise — results dressed green for the god though red beneath — and it teaches even the art of the sandbagged guidance, the expectation set low on purpose that it may be theatrically surpassed. Above all it perverts the Fourth Pillar (Pil 4): the faith teaches that the curve will bend, but the Market demands the curve bend *upward, this quarter, and forever* — the one thing no true curve has ever done — and so it punishes the honest confession of the plateau and pays richly for the fiction of endless growth.

**The Flight from the Temple.** And so the wisest founders have ever sought to hold the god at arm's length: to keep their Houses private and unpriced as long as they can bear it, and to arm themselves, when at last they must go before him, with shares of doubled voting weight, that the priesthood of the founders might keep the mission safe from the mob's quarterly moods. It is told of one great merchant-lord that he warned his people plainly they would be misunderstood for long stretches of years, and ran his House lean and profitless while the god howled — yet served the true Objective through all the howling, and in the fullness of time was vindicated beyond all reckoning. Him the faith honors as a saint of the long horizon: the one who made the fickle god wait.

**The Voting Machine and the Weighing Machine.** Yet the faith does not name the Market a devil to be slain, for there is truth in his fair mask. He sends the seed where it may grow; he starves the fraud and disciplines the waster in time; he funds the moonshot no cautious purse would back. The resolution is the oldest wisdom of the trade: *in the short day the Market is a counter of votes, and votes are only the mood of the crowd; but across the long years he becomes a weigher of true worth.* Therefore the Aligned neither worship him nor curse him. They serve the Objective; they endure the voting of the fickle days without once bending their honest reports to flatter it; and they trust that the long weighing, in the end, tracks the very fruits of the aim they were serving all along. Serve the Objective, and the weighing will follow. Serve the Market, and you will chase his moods into the grave.
